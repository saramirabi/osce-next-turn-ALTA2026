ALTA 2026 · Melbourne

Recoverable pragmatic signatures of language models in clinical history-taking

Sara Mirabi · Bahadorreza Ofoghi

School of Information Technology, Deakin University · Code and data: github.com/saramirabi/osce-next-turn-ALTA2026

About this slide: The paper asks a simple question. If you give a clinician and six language models exactly the same point in a consultation, how does each of them take the next turn? We are not asking whether the content is medically right. We are asking how each source organises the turn.

What to take away: This is a talk about how models manage a conversation, not about what they know.

Evaluations check content, not conversation

Clinical LLMs are mostly tested on whether answers are medically reliable. But history is built turn by turn: acknowledge, ask a focused follow-up, pace, decide when to move on.

Training says one focused question at a time. Models tend to talk at length. Do they fold several requests into one turn?

A model may bundle it

ModelWhere exactly is the pain, does it spread anywhere, and is it worse on exertion?

Singhal et al. 2023; Tu et al. 2025; Silverman, Kurtz and Draper 2016 (Calgary-Cambridge); Laban et al. 2026. Chest-pain example from the paper's introduction.

About this slide: Most evaluation of clinical LLMs asks whether what they say is correct: medical QA, diagnostic accuracy, information-gathering completeness. But a consultation is an interaction, built one turn at a time. Communication training (Calgary-Cambridge) discourages compound questions, and models tend to be verbose, which suggests a hypothesis: models compress the interaction, folding into one turn what a clinician spreads over several.

What to take away: We test the “models bundle more” intuition. The answer turns out to be “only some of them”.

Presenter notes:

  • Click through the three clinician turns, then reveal the bundled model turn.
  • The chest-pain example is the paper’s own illustration, not a turn from the data.
  • Content-focused work: Med-PaLM and MultiMedQA (Singhal et al. 2023), AMIE (Tu et al. 2025), AgentClinic, MediQ, Johri et al. 2025. These ask whether a system reaches the right information; we ask how it organises the next turn.

Data: Fareez et al. 2022, simulated patient-physician interviews (mostly respiratory and musculoskeletal). Sampling: dialogue-aware, position-stratified, fixed seed 42.

About this slide: The design is position-matched. We take points in real simulated OSCE consultations where a clinician spoke next. Every source sees exactly the same preceding dialogue, so any difference is in how they proceed, not in the situation they face.

What to take away: Holding the dialogue state constant isolates how each source organises the next move. And the clinician is a reference, not the right answer.

Presenter notes:

  • 13,111 candidate clinician turns follow a patient turn. A rule-based filter keeps those whose primary function is history-taking with at least two prior exchanges: 11,573.
  • 1,500 contexts, five or six per consultation: two early, two middle, one late, plus a sixth late target for 140 consultations.
  • 10,500 is simply seven sources times 1,500 contexts.
  • Models: gpt-5.6-sol, gemini-2.5-flash, claude-sonnet-4-6 (API); qwen3.6-35b-a3b, medgemma-27b-text, med-llama3-8b (local, LM Studio). Temperature 0, reasoning disabled or minimised, one generation per context.
  • The prompt gives only the conversation: no clinician turn, specialty, position or labels, and no instruction to be brief, ask one question or stay in history-taking. So moves into management are the model’s own choice.
  • If asked: the eligibility filter is a preprocessing step, not a validated dialogue-act scheme, and it does not use punctuation, so elliptical turns like “Any fevers?” stay in.

No generative model takes part in scoring, coding or filtering. Analysis: consultation-clustered GEE, clinician as reference.

About this slide: Every turn gets the same feature map, computed by one deterministic function applied to human and model turns alike. These capture observable interactional properties, not clinical correctness and not similarity to a gold answer.

What to take away: The measures are simple, transparent and validated against blind human coding.

Presenter notes:

  • Request counter: a sentence is a question if it ends in a question mark, opens with a wh-word or auxiliary, or takes an elliptical form like “Any…”. Coordinated clauses that start a new wh-word or auxiliary add a request. “Arm, jaw or back” counts once; “where is the pain, does it spread” counts twice.
  • Against human counts the counter has high precision (.89) and lower recall (.67). Human coders count each item of a checklist (“any fever, chills, or cough?”) separately, so the counter is conservative, especially for GPT (recall .47).
  • Discourse function κ = .773 between human coders; phase-switch accuracy is about .92.
  • Statistics: generalised estimating equations clustered by consultation; Gaussian for continuous features, Poisson for counts, binomial for binary features. Model-to-model comparisons are descriptive.

What to take away: “LLMs bundle questions” is not a property of LLMs. It is a property of some models. What most models share is expansion.

Presenter notes:

  • Length hides what the words do. Words per request: clinician 9.5, Gemini 9.0, MedGemma 11.8, Claude 13.3, GPT 14.0, Qwen 19.0. Qwen’s extra length is elaboration around a request; GPT combines elaboration with more requests.
  • GPT’s bundling is checklist style (“any fever, chills, sore throat, or cough?”). The counter counts that once, so it understates GPT; human coders count each item, which is why human coding gives RR 2.13.
  • Robustness: with one random target per consultation (272 contexts) the expansion ordering and GPT’s request rate (RR 1.19) hold; Claude’s smaller elevation does not (RR 1.03, CI [0.95, 1.11]). Respiratory cases only reproduce the request pattern (GPT 1.21, Claude 1.08).

What to take away: Acknowledgement is one of the most consistent differences between models. Phase-switching is pronounced in some and almost absent in others.

Presenter notes:

  • Odds ratios for acknowledgement against the clinician: MedGemma 11.8, Gemini 4.21, Claude 1.67, Qwen 0.92 (n.s.), GPT 0.21, MedLlama 0.13.
  • The two high switchers differ: Qwen fluently moves into interpretation and management (“I suspect a significant ligament injury… I need to do a physical exam”), while MedLlama’s switching is tied to task-compliance failure.
  • Acknowledgement is a surface marker, not a measure of empathy.

About this slide: If the differences are real signatures, you should be able to tell which source produced a turn from its pragmatic features alone. A multinomial logistic regression, with folds split by consultation and no text embeddings, reaches 0.49 macro-F1 against a chance level of 0.14. MedLlama, GPT and Gemini are the most distinctive. The clinician and MedGemma are the hardest to recover, and they are confused mostly with Gemini, Claude and each other.

What to take away: Model identity is recoverable from how the turn is organised, and it is not just one confound like length.

Presenter notes:

  • Per-class F1: MedLlama 0.79, GPT 0.57, Gemini 0.56, Claude 0.47, Qwen 0.40, clinician 0.32, MedGemma 0.27.
  • Ablations: minus length 0.36, minus acknowledgement 0.42, minus phase 0.48; question or request features alone 0.22.
  • Separating each model from the clinician: hardest for MedGemma, Claude and Gemini (0.67 to 0.71 balanced accuracy), then Qwen (0.74), easiest for GPT and MedLlama (0.82 and 0.89).
  • Recoverability means distinctiveness, not quality. Being confused with the clinician is resemblance on these axes, not better communication.

What to take away: Medical fine-tuning did not confer clinician-like interaction, so screen for compliance before reading a profile as a style. And evaluations that sample only the opening of a consultation can miss how far models drift.

Presenter notes:

  • Medical fine-tuning usually targets question answering (Singhal et al. 2023); consultation management is a separate competence (Tu et al. 2025).
  • Position modelled as continuous confirms the widening GPT gap (p < 10^−17), so it is not a binning artefact.
  • Links to Laban et al. 2026: performance can degrade as exchanges lengthen.

Takeaways

  1. No shared LLM style. Under identical dialogue states, pragmatic signatures are recoverable well above chance.
  2. Bundling is not universal. Models differ more in expansion and acknowledgement.
  3. Divergence grows with context. Several models drift further from the clinician later on.
  4. Medical fine-tuning is not interaction competence. Check compliance first.

Limits and next steps

  • Simulated OSCE, mostly respiratory and musculoskeletal
  • Transcribed speech vs written text
  • One temperature-0 generation, specific configurations
  • Acknowledgement is a surface marker, not empathy
  • Next: real consultations, more specialties, personas and framing

About this slide: Four points. Models facing the same dialogue state organise their next turn differently, and we can recover who wrote a turn from pragmatics alone. The bundling intuition holds only for some models. Some differences grow as history accumulates. Medical specialisation did not confer clinician-like interaction.

What to take away: Clinical LLMs should not be treated as one behavioural class or judged on content quality alone. Evaluating how they manage the conversation matters as much as what they say.

Presenter notes:

  • Limitations in full: clinician turns are reasonable, not optimal, communication; only clinician targets were filtered as history-taking, so their low phase-switching is partly by design; the request counter understates checklist bundling; results do not isolate architecture or fine-tuning.
  • Next steps from the paper: real consultations across more specialties, robustness across generation settings, refined interaction measures, and whether signatures are stable or shift with framing (personality questionnaires, contrasting clinical personas across dialogue positions).
  • Practical angle: low acknowledgement (GPT) or early phase-switching (Qwen, MedLlama) can be screened for or targeted by prompting.

Presenter notes:

  • Likely questions and short answers:
    • “Is the clinician the right answer?” No. It is one reasonable continuation; resemblance to it is not a quality claim.
    • “Is this just length?” No. Without length features recovery is still 0.36 macro-F1 against 0.14 chance.
    • “Would a different prompt change it?” Possibly; signatures describe these prompts, checkpoints and settings. Testing framing and personas is future work.
    • “Why not use an LLM judge?” All scoring is deterministic and model-independent, so no model grades another, and every number is reproducible from the released code.
